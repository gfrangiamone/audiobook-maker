Text to speech in 30 seconds

In short: a text to speech system (TTS, also called speech synthesis) turns written text into a spoken voice. First it rewrites the text the way it is said, then it works out how each word sounds and with what intonation, and finally a neural network generates the sound wave.

These slides walk through what happens between the text and the speaker, with a few technical deep dives and audio samples made with the voices of Audiobook Maker. At the end you will see what changes when the thing being read is an entire book.

Four generations of synthetic voices

  • Concatenative: snippets of recorded speech, cut and stitched together. A human voice, but with audible seams and stiff intonation.
  • Parametric: a statistical model describes the sound with a set of parameters and rebuilds it. Flexible, but muffled and “robotic”.
  • Neural: networks trained on thousands of hours of speech generate the sound almost sample by sample. This is the standard today, free voices included.
  • Language models: built like the large text models, they grasp the meaning of what they read and generate the audio directly. They can whisper, shift tone between characters and follow style instructions.

From text to sound, step by step

A classic speech synthesis system is a chain of stages. The newest models merge some of them, but the problems to solve are the same: the next slides take them one at a time.

1. Normalization: writing text the way it is said

Numbers, dates, times, abbreviations and symbols are not read the way they are written. The system first turns them into words, and the right reading depends on context: “Dr.” is “doctor” before a name and “drive” in an address; “Henry VIII” is “Henry the Eighth”, but “Chapter VIII” is “chapter eight”; “1/2” can be “one half” or “January second”.

Before the text reaches the voice, Audiobook Maker also strips out what should not be read aloud: page numbers, footnotes, running headers and words hyphenated at the end of a line.

2. From letters to phonemes: how it sounds

Next the system has to work out how each word sounds, turning letters (graphemes) into basic units of sound (phonemes). English spelling is famously irregular, and the same spelling can hide two different words: lead the metal and lead the way, read today and read yesterday, a live concert and the place where you live.

3. Prosody: intonation, rhythm and pauses

The same words can say different things. The difference lies in prosody: the voice rising in a question and falling in a statement, the rhythm, the pauses, the words that carry the stress. This, more than the timbre, is what makes a synthetic voice sound natural or mechanical.

The newest models also accept style instructions (“whispering”, “with irony”, “more slowly”): you will find how to write them in the guide to PREMIUM voices. Listen to a sentence that says the opposite of what it means: only the intonation gives the irony away.

4. The acoustic model: drawing the sound

The acoustic model takes the phonemes and the prosody and produces a picture of the sound to come, usually a spectrogram: a kind of musical score that says, moment by moment, how much energy there is in each frequency range.

Technical deep dive. The mel spectrogram splits frequencies into bands (often 80) spaced the way the ear hears them: dense in the lows, wide in the highs. Time is cut into frames of about ten milliseconds, roughly a hundred per second. One second of speech becomes a table of about 80 × 100 numbers: far easier to predict than the tens of thousands of values in the actual wave. The model also decides how many frames each phoneme gets, that is, how long it lasts: this is where rhythm comes from.

5. The vocoder and audio tokens

You cannot listen to a spectrogram. The vocoder turns it into a sound wave, the stream of numbers the speaker plays back: at 24 kHz that is 24,000 numbers for every second of speech. Neural vocoders are the reason today’s voices no longer have the metallic tone of older ones.

Technical deep dive. Many recent models skip the spectrogram: they predict the audio tokens of a neural codec directly, and the codec’s decoder turns them back into a sound wave. That is why they can laugh, hesitate or take a breath: these sounds have no phonetic transcription, but they do have tokens.

Voice cloning: a voice from a few seconds

Neural models separate what is said from who says it. A person’s timbre can be summed up in a kind of numerical fingerprint, taken from a short sample of their voice; the model can then read any text with that fingerprint. A few years ago this took hours of studio recordings. Today a few dozen seconds recorded on a phone are enough.

Why an audiobook is harder than a sentence

Almost every speech synthesis demo plays short sentences. A novel runs from 300,000 to over a million characters and lasts 5 to 20 hours: at this scale problems appear that a single sentence never shows.

Chunks and joins

No model reads a whole book in one go. The text is split into chunks, from a few hundred to a couple of thousand characters depending on the model, cut at the end of a sentence and never in the middle of a line of dialogue. The chunks are generated separately and then put back together.

Each chunk starts without “remembering” the tone of the one before: at the join the volume, speed or intonation can shift slightly, and the pause can come out too long or too short. Calibrated pauses and smoothed joins make them disappear.

Typical errors and how to catch them

Precisely because they generate sound instead of assembling it, neural models can fail in ways older systems never did:

  • repeating a word or skipping part of the text;
  • cutting off the end of a chunk or returning silence;
  • getting stuck in a loop on text that is hard to speak, such as tables of numbers;
  • switching language mid-sentence when they meet a foreign word.

In a book of a thousand chunks, even a rare error becomes likely. That is why every chunk has to be checked: its length must match the text, the audio must be neither silent nor clipped, and if something is off the chunk is regenerated. Another technique is to transcribe the audio automatically and compare it with the original text.

Chapters and the final file

Finally the chunks are joined into chapters and the result becomes an MP3 or an M4B file with chapters and a cover, so you can pick up listening exactly where you left off. In the free audiobook catalog you can hear the result on complete books, from Jane Austen to Jack London.

How the quality of a synthetic voice is measured

  • MOS (Mean Opinion Score): human listeners rate naturalness from 1 to 5. It is still the benchmark.
  • Word error rate: the audio is transcribed with speech recognition and the wrong, missing or extra words are counted. It measures intelligibility, not beauty.
  • Speaker similarity: for cloned voices, how close the fingerprint of the generated voice is to that of the sample.
  • Sample rate: how many values per second describe the wave.

Sample rate: 16, 24 or 48 kHz

Limits and ethics

A good synthetic voice is now hard to tell apart from a recording. That raises two issues.

  • Consent. Cloning someone’s voice without their permission is wrong and, in many countries, illegal. Use a cloned voice only if it is your own or if the person who lent it has agreed.
  • Transparency. Listeners have a right to know that a voice is synthetic. The audiobooks in our catalog say so, and the major distribution platforms require it.

One practical limit remains: a synthetic voice reads well what is written well. Tables, formulas, footnotes and complex layouts stay difficult for any model.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between text to speech and speech recognition?

They are opposite tasks. Text to speech (TTS) turns text into a voice; speech recognition (speech to text, STT) turns a voice into text, like dictation on your phone. They are often used together: to check synthetic audio, you transcribe it and compare it with the original text.

Why are numbers sometimes read wrong?

Because the right reading depends on context: a year, a price, a time and a page number are all read differently. If normalization misreads them, the voice correctly reads the wrong thing. Roman numerals and abbreviations are the trickiest cases.

Do synthetic voices understand what they read?

Not in the human sense, but recent models take the meaning of the sentence into account: that is how they choose the right pronunciation of a homograph or the intonation of a question. The larger the model, the more reliable this “understanding” is.

How long does it take to generate an audiobook?

Much less than the listening time, because the chunks are generated in parallel. A 300-page book is usually ready in under an hour, and processing continues on the server even if you close the page.

Can I get an audiobook read in my own voice?

Yes, with voice cloning: you record about 25 seconds, listen to two test readings and generate the book. Every step is in the guide audiobook in your own voice.

Are there speech synthesis systems that work offline?

Yes: phones and computers have built-in system voices that work without a connection, and there are open models you can install on your own computer. The most natural voices, though, need a lot of computing power and run on servers with dedicated graphics cards.

Glossary

Phoneme
The smallest unit of sound that tells two words apart, like the p and the b in “pat” and “bat”.
Normalization
Rewriting numbers, dates, abbreviations and symbols as words before synthesis.
Prosody
The intonation, rhythm, pauses and stress of a spoken sentence.
Spectrogram
Sound shown as energy per frequency over time; the “mel” kind follows how the ear perceives pitch.
Vocoder
The component that turns the spectrogram, or the audio tokens, into a sound wave.
Audio tokens
Discrete symbols into which a neural codec compresses audio, and which a model can predict like words.
Sample rate
The number of values per second that describe the sound wave: 16, 24 or 48 kHz.
Voice cloning
Creating a synthetic voice from a short sample of a real voice.
